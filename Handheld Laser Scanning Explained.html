How a handheld laser scanner measures a tree

The rotating head fires laser pulses, times their return and turns each echo into a point. Walk, and the points add up into a 3D tree. Switch the sun off: the scanner keeps working, because it brings its own light.

Light is slowed down about a hundred million times here so you can see it travel. Real pulses are near-infrared (often 905 nm) and invisible; they are drawn red. Pulses that hit nothing (sky) never come back and leave no point.

What it actually looks like

A handheld mobile laser scanner is not a torch you point at the tree. It is a spinning LiDAR head on a handle, which sprays pulses all around you while you walk, plus an inertial measurement unit (IMU) and a small computer that work out where the scanner was at every instant.

  • GeoSLAM ZEB Horizon: a puck-shaped multi-beam LiDAR on top of a handle; also mounts on a pole, backpack or drone.
  • Leica BLK2GO: a tall wireless wand with a dual-axis LiDAR dome and cameras that colour the points.
  • Low-cost builds: a compact sensor such as the Livox Mid-360 (about 265 g, 360° × 59° view, around 200,000 points per second, IMU built in) on a 3D-printed handle with a mini computer.

The head is usually tilted so its field of view catches the ground, the stems and the crown in one pass.

Why we call it an active sensor

An active sensor sends out its own energy and measures what comes back. A passive sensor, like a camera or a multispectral satellite, only records energy that already exists: reflected sunlight or emitted heat.

Passive: camera

Needs the sun. Under a dense canopy, at dusk or at night it gets little or nothing. It sees colour and texture, but distance has to be inferred afterwards (for example by photogrammetry).

Active: laser scanner

Brings its own light, so it works in shade and in darkness. Because it knows exactly when the pulse left, the return time gives the distance directly: every echo is already a 3D point.

The trade-offs: an active sensor needs power, has a limited range, must be eye-safe (Class 1 lasers), and the return strength depends on how well bark and leaves reflect at the laser's wavelength.

Two ways to turn light into distance

Pulsed time of flight

d =c · Δt2

Fire a short pulse, start a clock, stop it when the echo arrives. Divide by two because the light travels there and back. c ≈ 299,792,458 m/s.

Used by almost all mobile and handheld scanners (Velodyne, Ouster, Hesai, Livox) and long-range terrestrial scanners. One pulse can give several echoes, from leaves first and the stem behind.

Phase shift

Send a continuous beam whose brightness is modulated like a sine wave. The echo comes back delayed, so its wave is shifted. Measure that phase shift Δφ instead of a time.

Fast and precise at short range, but the phase repeats every λm/2, so a far target can be mistaken for a near one. Scanners combine several frequencies: a low one for coarse range, a high one for precision. Typical of high-speed terrestrial scanners such as the FARO Focus and Z+F IMAGER.

From one echo to a point in the forest

Each echo gives a range r and the beam's angles (θ, φ) inside the scanner. Because the scanner is moving, SLAM (scan matching plus the IMU) estimates its pose T(t) at the moment of each pulse, and the point is placed in one common frame:

p = T(t) · r · [cosφ cosθ, cosφ sinθ, sinφ]

Why walking helps

From one spot, the near sapling hides part of the big stem. As you walk, the beams reach it from new angles and the shadows fill in. That is why handheld scanning is quick in a forest plot: a loop around the plot sees every stem from several sides.

Notice too that the upper crown stays sparse: lower branches and leaves block the pulses from below. That is a real limit of below-canopy scanning, and why crown metrics from handheld data need care.
